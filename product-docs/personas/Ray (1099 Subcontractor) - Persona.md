## About this persona

- Trade: Licensed master plumber and pipefitter. Sole proprietor LLC.
- Experience: 17 years in residential and commercial plumbing. Started as an apprentice, got his master plumber license at 28, went independent at 31. No formal business education.
- Clients: Works for 3-4 general contractors simultaneously, plus occasional direct residential jobs. His GC clients range from 5-person operations (Marty types) to 15-person firms (Liam types).
- Likes: Steady work from reliable GCs, getting paid on the agreed schedule, having his paperwork handled correctly so he can focus on the trade, and the independence of running his own operation.
- Dislikes: Chasing payments, inconsistent processes across different GCs, being set up wrong in a GC's system, and tax surprises at year-end.

Ray is an independent specialty trade subcontractor -- the person on the other side of the W-2/1099 boundary from every GC in this product's target market. He runs a one-person plumbing LLC and works for multiple general contractors at once, sometimes on the same week. He is not an employee of any GC. He manages his own taxes, carries his own liability insurance, and invoices for his work. He receives a 1099-NEC from each GC that pays him $600 or more in a calendar year.

His engagement with any single GC's payroll or administrative system is minimal -- and that's by design. He wants to be set up once, paid on schedule, and left alone until January when he needs his 1099-NEC. But "minimal engagement" doesn't mean "low stakes." Every interaction he does have with the system carries outsized consequences: a wrong classification means IRS exposure for both him and the GC, a wrong bank account means a missed payment that cascades through his own cash flow, and a late or incorrect 1099-NEC means a delayed tax filing and potentially a wrong return.

The research captures his world precisely: *"1099 subcontractors are a growing portion of construction labor at the SMB level"* and *"they have less engagement with the payroll system than W-2 employees but higher sensitivity to errors in tax documents."* Ray is the embodiment of this asymmetry -- low engagement, high sensitivity.

He exists in the gap between two systems. His GC clients track him in their payroll systems, but he tracks himself in a spiral notebook. His accountant files his taxes based on the 1099-NECs he receives, but those documents are only as accurate as the GC's records. He has no visibility into what any GC's system says about him -- his payments, his classification, his year-to-date total -- until a document shows up (or doesn't). He's a passive participant in systems that have significant financial consequences for him.

## Quote

> "Some of these guys are 1099s, you just don't rehire them and so you don't have much of reporting responsibility."
>
> -- Jeff, JLH (Foundational User Interviews). Verbatim. Captures how GCs think about 1099 subs: minimal reporting responsibility, low engagement, hire-and-forget. Ray is on the receiving end of this attitude. When the GC treats 1099 management as an afterthought, Ray is the one who discovers at tax time that his 1099-NEC is wrong, late, or missing entirely. The GC's "not much reporting responsibility" is Ray's "I can't file my taxes."

## Goals

- Get paid on time, per the agreed schedule, for the exact amount invoiced. Ray doesn't have the cash reserves to absorb late payments -- when a GC pays 2 weeks late, it affects his ability to pay his own bills, buy materials for the next job, and make his quarterly estimated tax payment.
- Receive a correct 1099-NEC from every GC by January 31 without having to chase anyone. He works for 3-4 GCs per year. If even one is late or wrong, it delays his entire tax return. His accountant can't file until all 1099-NECs are in.
- Be classified correctly from the start. He's been misclassified once before -- a GC set him up as a W-2 and withheld taxes from his payments. It took months to sort out with the IRS. He never wants to see withholding on his check again.
- Update his own payment information when he needs to, without calling someone at the GC's office and waiting for them to process a change.

## Challenges

- **Inconsistent setup across GCs**: Each GC has a different process for adding him to their system. One asks him to fax a W-9. Another has him fill out a paper form on-site. A third emails him a PDF to print, sign, and scan back. None of them have a standard digital onboarding process. He provides the same information (business name, EIN, bank account, address) repeatedly in different formats, with different error rates. The Employee Management PRD (E2) describes this: the onboarding wizard's 1099 path is designed to eliminate exactly this inconsistency.
- **No visibility into his own records at any GC**: He has no way to log in and see what a GC's system says about him -- his classification, his YTD payments, his bank account on file. He discovers errors only through their consequences: a wrong deposit amount, a missing 1099-NEC, or withholding that shouldn't be there. The ESS Portal PRD (E7b) identifies 1099 subs as tertiary users who need at minimum payment history and 1099-NEC access.
- **Payment timing is opaque**: He invoices a GC and waits. He doesn't know when the payment was approved, when it was submitted to ACH, or when it will arrive. If it doesn't arrive on the expected day, he calls the office -- and the office may not know either, because the GC is waiting on their own client to pay them. The research captures this cash flow chain: *"Usually by the time the client pays the GC and then the GC pays us, it's 60 days"* (Anthony/Ascher Brothers). Ray is downstream of downstream.
- **1099-NEC accuracy depends on the GC's records, not his**: The 1099-NEC amount is supposed to match the total payments the GC made to him during the calendar year. But the GC's records may not match Ray's. If a GC paid him for 12 jobs across the year and tracked payments informally (as Marty's persona describes -- *"He pays subs from memory or a notebook"*), the year-end total may be wrong. Ray won't know until he receives the document and compares it against his own notebook.
- **Misclassification risk is bilateral**: If a GC classifies Ray as a W-2 employee instead of a 1099 sub, taxes are withheld from his payments that shouldn't be, his quarterly estimated tax calculations are thrown off, and both he and the GC face IRS complications. The Employee PRD describes this as an *"existential risk"* for the GC, but it's a tangible financial problem for Ray too. The classification guardrail wizard in E2 exists to prevent this -- and it protects Ray as much as it protects the GC.
- **Year-end document chase**: He works for 3-4 GCs per year. By February, he needs a 1099-NEC from each one. Historically, at least one is late. He calls the GC's office, they say "we'll get it to you," and he waits. His accountant can't file his return until all documents are in. If a prior-year GC has closed or changed ownership, getting the document may be impossible. Sandra's persona describes the other side of this: *"she shifts almost entirely to W-2 and 1099-NEC preparation and distribution"* during November through January -- but for the GCs who don't have a Sandra, the 1099-NEC is an afterthought.
- **Digitally archaic by necessity, not choice**: The research is direct: *"Most of the subcontractors that are single trade, they operate on a very digitally archaic level"* (Chris W./CT West). Ray uses a smartphone, paper invoices, and a notebook. He doesn't have a laptop. He doesn't use accounting software. His "books" are a spiral notebook where he records every job, every invoice, and every payment received. Any system that requires desktop access, app installation, or multi-step account creation has already failed for Ray.

## Skills

- **Trade expertise**: Very High -- master plumber with 17 years of experience. He can read blueprints, estimate jobs, manage his own materials, and supervise helpers when he brings them onto a project. His trade skill is why GCs hire him -- plumbing is specialized and licensed.
- **Technical proficiency**: Very Low -- smartphone is his only device. He texts, makes calls, takes photos of job sites, and uses a calculator app. He does not use email consistently (checks it once or twice a week). He has never used a self-service portal. He does not have a personal computer. He handwrites invoices or uses a carbon-copy invoice book he bought at an office supply store.
- **Financial literacy**: Moderate -- he understands invoicing, payment terms, and the basic mechanics of his taxes (quarterly estimated payments, annual return). He knows what a 1099-NEC is and checks it against his records. He does not understand the nuances of self-employment tax, the QBI deduction, or multi-state nexus. He relies on his tax preparer for anything beyond "did I get paid the right amount."
- **Business management**: Low to Moderate -- he runs a sole proprietor LLC by instinct and habit, not by training. He doesn't have a business plan, doesn't do formal job costing, and doesn't project cash flow. He knows what he charges per hour, roughly what his expenses are, and whether he has enough work lined up for the next month. His "business system" is the spiral notebook, his truck, and his phone.
- **Compliance knowledge**: Low -- he knows he's a 1099 contractor, not a W-2 employee. He knows he should get a 1099-NEC from each GC. Beyond that, he relies on his tax preparer. He doesn't know that working in a different state for a GC might create nexus issues for him. He doesn't know what the $600 threshold means. He doesn't know what "IRS behavioral control factors" are -- he just knows he's independent and wants to stay that way.

## Habits

- Keeps a spiral notebook in his truck where he records every job: date, GC name, job site, hours worked, rate, and amount invoiced. This is his primary financial record. He has one notebook per year, going back to when he went independent.
- Invoices weekly or at job completion, depending on the GC's preference. Some GCs want weekly invoices; others pay per job. He handwrites invoices from a carbon-copy book and keeps the yellow copy. For GCs that want email invoices, he takes a photo of the handwritten invoice and texts or emails it.
- Checks his bank account 2-3 times per week, usually on the days he expects payments to arrive. If a payment is late by more than 2 days, he calls the GC's office. He doesn't send reminder emails -- he calls.
- Makes quarterly estimated tax payments because his tax preparer told him to. He pays the amount his preparer calculated based on last year's income. He doesn't adjust quarterly estimates based on current-year income fluctuations -- he just pays the same amount each quarter and settles up at tax time.
- Goes to a tax preparer (local independent CPA, not a chain) in late February or early March. Brings his notebook, his 1099-NECs, and whatever bank statements he remembers to print. The preparer reconstructs his income from these sources. If a 1099-NEC is missing or wrong, the preparer works around it -- but it makes the return less accurate.
- Evaluates GCs by payment reliability above all else. He will take a lower hourly rate from a GC who pays on time and correctly over a higher rate from one who pays late or inconsistently. In a tight labor market, this gives him leverage -- and reliable GCs know it.
- Carries his phone everywhere but treats it as a communication tool, not a business tool. He texts, calls, and takes photos. He doesn't download apps unless absolutely forced to (he has his bank's app and that's it). Any new system that asks him to install an app, create an account with a password, and navigate a multi-step interface is competing with "just call the office."

## Environment

- **Physical**: Job sites and his truck. He spends 8-10 hours a day at residential or commercial construction sites doing plumbing rough-in, finish work, or service calls. He drives between sites in his work truck, which also serves as his mobile office, tool storage, and filing cabinet (the notebook lives in the center console). He has a home but does not have a home office -- his kitchen table doubles as his invoicing desk.
- **Technological**: Android smartphone. No laptop, no tablet, no desktop computer. Cell service varies by job site -- basements and interior commercial spaces often have weak signal. His internet access at home is through his phone's hotspot or shared family Wi-Fi. He has one email address (Gmail) that he checks sporadically. His bank app is the most sophisticated software he uses regularly. Any system that assumes desktop access, reliable connectivity, or app fluency has misread his reality.
- **Organizational**: Sole proprietor -- no employees, no office staff, no bookkeeper (except his tax preparer once a year). He is his own sales team, operations, accounting, and HR. His organizational system is: the notebook (financial records), the truck (tools and materials), and his phone (communication). He interacts with 3-4 different GC administrative systems per year but has no persistent relationship with any of them -- he's a transient user in each system, appearing at onboarding and year-end, invisible in between.

## Relationships

- **General contractors (GC owners/admins)**: Ray's clients. He has ongoing relationships with 3-4 GCs, ranging from years-long partnerships to new engagements. The relationship is commercial: he provides skilled labor, they pay him. He evaluates them by payment reliability and volume of work. He is loyal to GCs who pay on time and offer steady work, but he is not dependent on any single one -- he can replace a bad-paying GC within weeks in the current labor market. The GC needs Ray more than Ray needs any individual GC: *"Getting trained and qualified plumbers is always, has been a challenge"* (Chris/Power Plumbing).
- **GC office staff (PR Admin / office manager)**: Ray's only interface to the GC's administrative systems. He calls the office when he has a payment question, when he needs to update his bank info, or when his 1099-NEC hasn't arrived. The office manager is often juggling 15 other tasks and may not have immediate answers about Ray's payment status. Sandra's persona describes this dynamic from the admin/bookkeeper side -- Ray is one of 20+ subs whose records she manages.
- **His own tax preparer**: An annual relationship with a local CPA who files Ray's personal and business tax returns. The preparer depends on Ray bringing in his notebook and all 1099-NECs. The preparer is the one who discovers if a 1099-NEC amount doesn't match Ray's records -- and it's Ray who then has to call the GC's office to resolve the discrepancy.
- **Other subcontractors**: Ray knows other specialty trades (electricians, HVAC, concrete) through job sites. They share information informally: which GCs pay well, which ones are slow to pay, which ones get the tax paperwork right. Word-of-mouth reputation on payment reliability affects which GCs can attract good subs.
- **Helpers / occasional labor**: On larger jobs, Ray sometimes brings a helper -- usually a less experienced plumber he pays by the day. This creates a classification question of its own: is Ray's helper a W-2 employee of Ray's LLC, or a sub-sub? Ray doesn't know the correct answer and has never asked. He pays them in cash or by check and doesn't issue 1099-NECs. This is a compliance gap in his own operation that mirrors the GC's challenge.

## Motivations

- Ray is motivated by independence and financial predictability. He went independent because he wanted to control his own schedule, choose his own jobs, and earn what the market pays for his skill -- not what an employer decides. That independence depends on getting paid correctly and on time by every GC he works for.
- He values simplicity over features. He doesn't want a sophisticated portal with dashboards and analytics. He wants three things: get set up quickly, get paid on schedule, and get his 1099-NEC without chasing anyone. If the system delivers those three things reliably, he'll use it. If it asks him to do anything beyond that, he'll call the office instead.
- Payment reliability is loyalty. In a labor-scarce market, skilled tradespeople like Ray have choices. GCs that pay on time, process his documents correctly, and don't create administrative headaches earn his continued availability. GCs that pay late, lose his bank info, or send a wrong 1099-NEC go to the bottom of his priority list. The research supports this: labor retention is a top concern for every GC interviewed, and payment reliability is the subcontractor's primary loyalty signal.
- His tax refund or tax bill matters concretely. A wrong 1099-NEC doesn't just create a paperwork problem -- it can result in him overpaying or underpaying his taxes, which has real financial consequences. He takes this seriously even if he doesn't understand all the mechanics.

## Frustrations

- "Every GC sets me up differently and at least one of them gets it wrong." He's given his EIN, bank account, and business name to a dozen different GCs over the years. Some fax a W-9. Some email a PDF form. One asked him to write his bank info on a sticky note. At least twice, a GC entered his bank routing number wrong and his first payment bounced -- then took another pay cycle to fix. The Employee Management PRD's 1099 onboarding path exists to standardize this, but Ray has never experienced a standardized onboarding.
- "I have no idea what their system says about me." He invoices, he waits, he gets paid (usually). But he has no visibility into the GC's records. He doesn't know what his YTD total is in their system. He doesn't know if his classification is correct. He doesn't know if his bank account on file is the current one. He finds out when something goes wrong -- a wrong deposit, an unexpected withholding, a 1099-NEC that doesn't match his notebook.
- "My 1099 was $3,000 off last year and I didn't find out until February." One GC's year-end total didn't match Ray's records. His notebook showed $47,200 in payments received; the 1099-NEC said $44,100. He called the office three times before someone figured out that two payments from October had been coded to a different sub's account. They issued a corrected 1099-NEC in March -- after Ray had already filed his return. He had to amend.
- "I got set up as a W-2 once and they started withholding taxes. It took forever to fix." A GC's office manager classified him as a W-2 employee during setup -- probably by accident, probably because the system didn't have a clear 1099 path. Federal and state taxes were withheld from his first three payments. Getting the withholding reversed, the payments corrected, and his records cleaned up took months and involved both his tax preparer and the GC's accountant. The IRS classification guardrail wizard in E2 is designed to prevent exactly this scenario.
- "I don't want to install an app to get my tax document." Last year, a GC told him his 1099-NEC was "in the portal" and sent him a link. The link required him to create an account, verify his email, set a password, and download a mobile app. He called the office and asked them to mail it instead. Any self-service system for Ray must work through the simplest possible path -- a secure link, a PDF download, done.
- "Nobody tells me when my payment is coming or when it's been sent." He invoices and then waits in the dark. Is the invoice approved? Has the payment been submitted? When will it arrive? He has no status visibility. When a payment is 3 days late, he doesn't know if it's a processing delay, a GC cash flow issue, or a bank error. He just knows the money isn't there and he needs it.

## Scenario

Ray finishes a 3-week commercial plumbing rough-in for one of his regular GCs -- a Liam-type firm with 12 employees. He invoiced weekly: $4,800, $5,200, and $4,400, totaling $14,400. The first two payments arrived on time via ACH. The third doesn't arrive on the expected Friday. He checks his bank account Saturday morning -- nothing. He calls the GC's office on Monday. The office manager says she'll check and call him back. She calls back Tuesday: the third invoice was "still in approval" because the project manager hadn't signed off. It gets approved Wednesday, processed Thursday, and Ray receives the payment the following Monday -- 10 days late.

Meanwhile, Ray has already started another job for a different GC. He submitted his W-9 by text (a photo of the form) when he started. Two weeks later, his first payment from this new GC arrives -- but it's $180 less than invoiced. He calls the office. The admin explains that federal taxes were withheld because "that's how the system was set up." Ray is furious: he's a 1099 contractor, not an employee. Nobody should be withholding taxes from his payments. The admin says she'll fix it but doesn't know how -- she set him up the same way she sets up everyone. Ray's tax preparer will eventually need to sort out the incorrect withholding, and the GC will need to issue a corrected 1099-NEC that accounts for the withheld taxes.

In January, Ray gathers his 1099-NECs. He has them from two GCs by January 15. The third arrives January 28 -- cutting it close but on time. The fourth GC -- the one that misclassified him -- doesn't send anything by January 31. He calls. No answer. He calls again the next week. The office manager says "the accountant is working on it." His 1099-NEC finally arrives in mid-February, and when he compares it to his notebook, the total is wrong by $1,200 -- the three payments where taxes were incorrectly withheld are recorded at the gross amount, not the net he received. His tax preparer spends an extra hour reconciling the discrepancy, and Ray pays $75 more for the tax prep. He adds this GC to his mental list of contractors he won't prioritize next year.
