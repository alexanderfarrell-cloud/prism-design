## About this persona

- GAR: 7.5M
- Employees: 3
- Experience: 5 years of experience as a business owner. Some accounting skills. BA in Architecture.
- Likes: Selling new jobs, excelling in his expertise, and seeing projects finish on time.
- Dislikes: Paying for underutilized Quickbooks, is disappointed by mediocre 'all-in-one' solutions, and sees accounting as a necessary evil.

Liam is responsible for creating bids and quotes, purchasing and expense tracking, customer invoicing, payroll, and project management. He meets with new and existing customers, manages a small team, and strives for healthy profit margins on jobs. He is responsible for ensuring that financial data is captured, although he doesn't like accounting and would rather be doing anything else.

His 3 employees include 1-2 salaried architects and an hourly admin/support person. Payroll is handled through his accountant using QuickBooks Payroll -- Liam doesn't touch the payroll run himself. He occasionally hires 1099 subcontractors for specialized work (structural engineers, MEP consultants) and his accountant processes those payments. As a design-build firm, his projects sometimes span state lines, creating multi-state payroll exposure he hasn't yet had to confront.

## Quote

> "I hate taxes. My accounting department sends the necessary information to my outside accountant. I don't really get involved beyond that."
>
> -- Composite from Segment A interviews (Tom/CarrHomes, Joe/Sainte Claire, Jeff H./Hammerwell). Represents the delegation-as-avoidance pattern observed in 12/27 participants.

## Goals

- To own a successful design-build business. To streamline administrative work, grow the business, and maintain cash flow.
- Run payroll accurately and on time without becoming a payroll expert. Stay compliant with tax obligations without understanding the underlying rules. He wants a safety net, not more features to learn.

## Challenges

- He struggles to find an affordable accounting solution for job phases and relies on his accountant to adapt QuickBooks. Finding an accountant willing to work outside QuickBooks was challenging. Both he and his accountant face inefficiencies from using multiple systems to manage the business.
- No visibility into true labor cost per project. QuickBooks Payroll posts gross wages but not fully burdened cost (employer taxes, benefits). Liam can't determine real project profitability from labor data alone -- and labor is his largest project expense.
- Compliance anxiety. He relies entirely on his accountant for tax filings and deposits. If his accountant misses a deadline or files incorrectly, Liam bears the penalty. He outsources payroll for liability protection, not efficiency.
- Approaching the Scale-Up Cliff. At 3 employees, payroll is manageable. If Liam hires a 4th or 5th employee, takes a project in another state, or brings on a W-2 field worker, his current setup (accountant + QuickBooks) will start breaking. He doesn't yet know what he doesn't know.

## Skills

- **Domain expertise**: High -- deeply skilled in architecture and construction management. Understands project phases, bidding, and client relationships.
- **Technical proficiency**: Moderate -- comfortable with familiar tools (SketchUp, Excel, Google Drive) but avoids learning new software unless the pain becomes acute. Prefers tools that work the way he already thinks.
- **Financial literacy**: Low to Moderate -- understands revenue, expenses, and profit margins at a project level. Cannot read a payroll tax breakdown or explain the difference between gross wages and fully burdened labor cost. Relies on his accountant to interpret financial detail.
- **Compliance knowledge**: Low -- unaware of quarterly 941 filing deadlines, SUTA obligations, or new-hire reporting requirements. His mental model is "my accountant handles all of that."

## Habits

- He relies on an external accountant to catch errors and flag concerns while handling taxes. He expects proactive communication about financial issues. He manually transfers data from invoices and expenses into Quickbooks.
- Liam prefers his spreadsheets and manual entry over complex accounting systems. He's considered an affordable system tailored to job phases.
- His accountant runs payroll every two weeks through QuickBooks. Liam reviews the output but doesn't understand the tax line items.
- He tracks hours informally -- salaried staff are "just there" and the hourly employee logs time on a shared spreadsheet.
- At year-end, his accountant generates W-2s and any 1099-NECs. Liam signs what he's told to sign.
- He occasionally Googles payroll tax questions when he sees a number that looks wrong, but rarely acts on what he finds.

## Environment

- **Physical**: Primarily office-based in a small studio. Occasional job site visits for design-build projects, but the majority of his work happens at a desk. His office doubles as a meeting room for client presentations.
- **Technological**: Laptop, smartphone, reliable internet. Runs SketchUp for design, Excel for tracking, QuickBooks for accounting, Google Drive for file sharing. No dedicated project management or payroll software -- the "stack" is a collection of general-purpose tools pressed into service. Matches the pattern observed across Segment A: *"We use a little bit of that and spreadsheets and email and cell phone -- kind of whatever works best in whatever situation"* (Jeff H./Hammerwell).
- **Organizational**: Informal culture. No written HR policies, no employee handbook, no formal onboarding process. Decisions are made quickly because Liam is the sole decision-maker. The pace is driven by project deadlines, not administrative calendars.

## Relationships

- **External accountant/CPA**: The most influential relationship in Liam's financial life. Dictates the accounting platform (QuickBooks), runs payroll, files taxes, generates W-2s and 1099-NECs. Liam trusts this person implicitly but cannot verify their work. The accountant is both a safety net and a single point of failure. This pattern was observed in 22/27 interview participants -- CPAs constrain software adoption and own the compliance relationship.
- **Employees (3)**: Directly affected by payroll accuracy and timeliness. They bring questions about paychecks and withholding to Liam, who cannot answer them. Liam feels responsible for their wellbeing but lacks the tools to demonstrate that responsibility through transparent compensation.
- **1099 subcontractors**: Occasional relationship -- structural engineers, MEP consultants. Liam views them as vendor relationships, not workforce management. He doesn't track payments systematically; his accountant reconstructs records at year-end.
- **Clients**: The relationship Liam cares most about. He measures his professional identity through client satisfaction and project quality -- not through administrative competence.

## Motivations

- Liam is motivated by creative and professional excellence. He started an architecture firm to design buildings, not to manage payroll. Administrative work is tolerated as the cost of independence.
- He wants to feel in control of his business without having to understand every operational detail. The ideal state is confidence that "someone has it handled" -- currently his accountant, ideally a system.
- Financial security for his employees matters to him. He expanded the bookkeeper's role description first (matching Joe/Sainte Claire: *"I probably expand my bookkeeper first and then the field person so I can take bookkeeping off of my table"*). He sees taking care of his team as part of being a good boss, even though he can't articulate how payroll fits into that.

## Frustrations

- "I'm paying for features I don't use." QuickBooks feels bloated for his needs, but his accountant requires it. He's trapped in a tool that doesn't match his mental model. This mirrors the "reluctant default" pattern from Findings Theme 3 (18/27 participants).
- He can't answer a simple employee question about their paycheck without calling his accountant and waiting two days. The dependency makes him feel incompetent in front of his own team.
- Year-end taxes feel like a black box. He signs what he's told to sign. He doesn't know if his accountant is doing an excellent job or a mediocre one -- he has no frame of reference.
- He suspects he's not making as much money on projects as he thinks, but he can't prove it because labor costs are opaque. This maps directly to Findings Theme 4: job costing as the "holy grail nobody has cracked" (24/27 participants).

## Scenario

When Liam hires a new junior designer (employee #4), he needs to set up payroll for them -- W-4, direct deposit, state withholding. He calls his accountant, who adds the employee to QuickBooks Payroll manually. Two weeks later, the new employee asks why their paycheck seems low. Liam has no way to verify the withholding is correct. He forwards the question to his accountant and waits two days for an answer. He feels embarrassed that he can't resolve a simple employee question himself -- and anxious that this gap in his knowledge will only widen as the firm grows.
