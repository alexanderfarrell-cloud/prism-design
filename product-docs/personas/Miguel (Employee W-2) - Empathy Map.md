# Empathy Map: Miguel (Employee W-2)

## Context

Miguel is a 34-year-old carpenter/framer and the field worker archetype -- the person on the receiving end of every payroll decision. He works 2-3 job sites per week at different rates, may cross state lines without knowing the tax implications, and verifies every paycheck through mental math. He has zero self-service access to his payroll information. Every question, update, or document request routes through the office. His experience of the payroll system is mediated entirely through two outputs: his paycheck deposit and his pay stub. His trust in the employer is directly proportional to pay accuracy and transparency.

---

## Says

- "My check was short again -- I know because I keep a running total in my head."
- "I worked 32 hours framing and 16 on crane. The stub just says 'Regular' and 'Overtime.' Which hours are which?"
- "I changed my bank account three weeks ago and the money still went to the old one."
- "Nobody told me we were working in another state. Now there's a new tax line on my stub and I don't know what it is."
- "I just want to see the math. If you show me how you got the number, I'll know if it's right."
- "Every time I start at a new company, the first two paychecks are wrong. It's always something."
- "Why do I have to call the office to get a copy of my own pay stub?"
- "I don't care how the system works. I care that the number in my bank account is correct."
- "My W-2 wasn't ready until the end of February. That's a month late for my taxes."
- "I asked the office about my overtime rate and she said she'd call me back. That was Monday. It's Friday."

## Does

- Mentally calculates expected gross pay by Thursday evening -- tallying hours at each rate, estimating overtime, arriving at a number he compares against his deposit on Friday morning.
- Checks his bank account first thing on payday. If the deposit doesn't match his mental number, he starts making calls before 8 AM.
- Asks his foreman about pay discrepancies first, even though the foreman can't answer payroll questions. The foreman becomes an unreliable intermediary who may give incorrect explanations.
- Calls the office when his pay is wrong -- but avoids calling for anything else. He'd rather live with incorrect withholding than go through the process of updating his W-4 by paper form.
- Fills out paper forms (W-4, direct deposit) by copying what he did last time. Doesn't read the instructions. Guesses on fields he doesn't understand (like "multiple jobs adjustment").
- Compares pay with co-workers informally. "What did you get this week?" is a routine conversation in the truck. Differences create suspicion even when they're explained by different withholding elections or rates.
- Loses paper documents -- W-4 forms, pay stubs, direct deposit slips end up in the truck, go through the wash, or get buried in a pile.
- Goes to H&R Block in February with whatever documents he has. If his W-2 is late or missing, he delays filing and potentially delays his refund -- money he's counting on.
- Browses job postings on his phone when pay issues accumulate. Doesn't threaten to quit, just quietly looks. When the right opportunity appears, he takes it.
- Accepts pay explanations he doesn't understand. Nods when the office says "that's how overtime works" but doesn't actually believe the explanation. The doubt accumulates silently.

## Thinks

- "I'm pretty sure I'm being shorted on overtime, but I can't prove it because the stub doesn't show the breakdown."
- "Why is this so complicated? I worked X hours at Y rate. Just pay me X times Y."
- "Every time I switch companies, it takes a month before my paycheck is right. That's a month of stress I don't need."
- "I shouldn't have to call someone to change my own bank account. That's my information."
- "If they're making mistakes on my check, what else are they getting wrong? Are my taxes right? Is my W-2 going to be correct?"
- "The guys on my crew who do the same hours are getting different amounts. Something doesn't add up."
- "Nobody explains anything. I just get a stub with numbers and I'm supposed to figure it out."
- "I bet there's a way to do this where I can just see everything on my phone. Other companies must have that."
- "My employer probably doesn't even know my overtime is being calculated wrong. They just trust whatever QuickBooks spits out."
- "Tax season is always stressful because I never know if my documents are going to be right."

## Feels

- **Frustrated** when his pay stub obscures the calculation behind summary lines. He knows the inputs (hours and rates) and he knows the output (net pay) but the middle is a black box. He can't verify the math because the math isn't shown.
- **Disrespected** when pay issues take days to resolve. He showed up every day, did the work, and submitted his hours. If the employer can't pay him correctly, that feels like his labor isn't valued.
- **Anxious** about multi-state withholding he doesn't understand. A new tax line appeared on his stub without explanation. He doesn't know if he owes more at year-end, if he's being double-taxed, or if this is normal. The uncertainty sits with him until April.
- **Powerless** because he has no direct access to any of his own information. He can't view his pay history, change his bank account, update his W-4, or download his W-2 without going through the office. Every request is a dependency on someone else's schedule and competence.
- **Suspicious** when co-workers report different pay for apparently similar work. He doesn't understand withholding differences well enough to explain the gap, and nobody else explains it either.
- **Resigned** to onboarding errors. He expects his first 2-3 paychecks at a new employer to be wrong. He's internalized this as "how it works" rather than a solvable problem.
- **Loyal (conditionally)** to employers who pay correctly and consistently. Pay accuracy is the primary trust signal. When it breaks, loyalty erodes faster than any other factor.
- **Stressed** at tax time. His refund is a significant financial event -- he plans for it. A late W-2, an incorrect one, or an unexpected multi-state filing requirement disrupts his annual financial plan.

---

## Goals and Needs

**At the center of everything Miguel wants is this: transparency and control over his own pay information.**

- A pay stub that shows the math -- hours broken out by rate and job site, overtime calculated visibly with the weighted average method shown, deductions itemized with enough context to understand why.
- Self-service access from his phone to change his direct deposit, update his W-4, view current and historical pay stubs, and download his W-2 -- without calling anyone.
- Proactive notification when something changes -- a new state withholding, a rate adjustment, a failed direct deposit -- before he discovers it by checking his bank account.
- Onboarding that gets it right the first time -- digital entry of his W-4 and direct deposit information with validation, so the first paycheck at a new employer is correct.
- A historical record of his own documents that survives employer changes -- pay stubs and W-2s accessible even if the employer closes or he leaves.

---

## Analysis

### Says vs. Thinks Gap

Miguel says "my check was short" but thinks "I might be getting systematically underpaid on overtime and I have no way to verify it." The gap is significant: what he voices as an isolated complaint may actually be a pattern of calculated distrust. He doesn't accuse the employer of intentional underpayment -- he suspects systemic error that nobody is catching because nobody is showing the math.

He says "I'll figure it out" when the office gives an incomplete explanation, but he thinks "that didn't actually make sense and I still don't know if I was paid correctly." He stops asking not because he's satisfied, but because the interaction cost (calling the office, waiting for a callback, getting a non-answer) exceeds the perceived benefit. This creates a growing deficit of unresolved pay questions that silently erode trust.

He tells co-workers "the check was short again" but doesn't tell the employer he's browsing job postings. The employer has no signal that pay transparency is a retention issue until Miguel gives notice.

### Key Tensions

1. **High financial awareness vs. low information access**: Miguel can calculate his expected gross in his head but can't verify it against the system's calculation because the pay stub doesn't show the inputs. He has the skill to audit his own pay -- the system just doesn't give him the data to do it.

2. **Mobile-only user vs. no mobile experience**: Miguel's entire digital life runs through his phone. The payroll system offers him nothing mobile-accessible -- no self-service portal, no digital pay stubs optimized for a phone screen, no way to update his information without paper. The product assumes a desktop user who doesn't exist in Miguel's world.

3. **Multi-rate, multi-site complexity vs. single-line-item stubs**: Miguel's pay is genuinely complex -- different rates, different jobs, different states, weighted average overtime. His pay stub presents this complexity as a single "Regular" and "Overtime" line. The simplification that was meant to make the stub readable actually makes it unverifiable.

4. **Employer retention goals vs. invisible attrition signal**: The employer wants to retain skilled workers like Miguel (labor shortage is a constant pressure). Miguel's loyalty is conditional on pay accuracy and transparency. But the employer gets no signal that pay opacity is causing attrition risk -- Miguel doesn't complain loudly, he just leaves.

5. **Compliance obligations Miguel doesn't know he has**: Working across state lines triggers withholding requirements that neither Miguel nor his employer may fully understand. Miguel discovers this through unexplained deductions, not through proactive communication. The compliance system needs to inform the employee, not just the admin.

### Design Implications

1. **Pay stub transparency with calculation drill-down**: The pay stub must show earnings broken out by rate, job site, and trade type. Overtime must display the WAOT calculation visibly: which hours at which rates contributed to the weighted average, what the resulting OT rate is, and how it was applied. This is the single highest-impact feature for Miguel's trust. The ESS Portal and Pay Stubs PRDs both identify this as a critical requirement -- the stub should be *verifiable*, not just readable.

2. **Mobile-first employee self-service portal**: Every feature Miguel needs -- viewing pay stubs, changing direct deposit, updating W-4, downloading W-2 -- must be designed for a smartphone screen with unreliable connectivity. The interface must work in 2-3 taps, load fast on poor cell signal, and require no desktop fallback. Progressive web app or responsive design is non-negotiable. Offline access to recent pay stubs would address the job-site connectivity problem.

3. **Self-service for direct deposit and W-4 updates with validation**: Miguel should be able to update his bank account and withholding elections directly from his phone, with immediate validation (routing number verification, W-4 logic checks) and clear confirmation of when the change will take effect. The current paper-form-to-office-entry-to-next-pay-cycle pipeline introduces errors at every handoff. Removing the intermediary eliminates the error source.

4. **Proactive multi-state withholding notifications**: When Miguel is assigned to a job site in a new state, the system should notify him -- in plain language, on his phone -- that a new state withholding will appear on his next stub, what it means, and whether his home-state withholding has been adjusted. This transforms a confusing pay-stub surprise into an expected, explained change. The notification should happen *before* the paycheck, not after.

5. **Onboarding that validates at entry**: Digital onboarding for new employees should capture W-4 and direct deposit information with real-time validation -- flagging common errors (wrong routing number, inconsistent withholding elections) before the first payroll runs. Getting the first paycheck right is the strongest trust signal the product can send to a new employee like Miguel.

6. **Historical document access that survives employment changes**: Miguel should be able to access his pay stubs and W-2s from any prior employer that used the platform, even after leaving the company. This addresses the "prior employer closed their office" scenario and reduces Miguel's dependency on companies he no longer works for. A persistent employee identity across employers is a significant differentiator.

7. **Trust through transparency, not simplification**: The design instinct is often to simplify the pay stub. For Miguel, simplification is the problem -- it hides the math he needs to verify. The right approach is *progressive disclosure*: a clean summary that looks simple, with the ability to tap into each line item to see the full calculation. Complexity should be available on demand, not removed entirely.
