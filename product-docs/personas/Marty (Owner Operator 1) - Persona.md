## About this persona

- GAR: <5M
- Employees: 1
- Experience: Years of hands-on construction experience, certified electrician
- Likes: Working with his hands, job variety, client satisfaction, and being his own boss
- Dislikes: Paperwork, accounting, and chasing down late payments

Marty wears many hats, managing both the hands-on work and business operations. He finds new jobs and handles pricing, tracks expenses, and keeps an eye on profitability. He transports tools and materials to the job site, takes care of waste disposal, and ensures work is completed on schedule and meets expectations. His financial responsibilities include invoicing and cash flow management. To sustain and grow his business, he markets his services and maintains necessary licenses and permits.

As a sole proprietor, Marty pays himself via owner's draw -- he has no formal payroll. When jobs require extra hands, he hires 1099 subcontractors (electricians, helpers) and pays them via check or direct bank transfer. He does not consistently file 1099-NECs for subs; his accountant scrambles to reconstruct payment records at tax time. He has considered hiring a full-time helper (W-2 employee) but is deterred by the perceived complexity of "real payroll" -- tax withholding, deposits, filings, and workers' comp feel like an impenetrable wall.

## Quote

> "Owner, pretty much whatever needs to be done from estimating to admin to scheduling to day to day to site meetings, whatever."
>
> -- Jodi, JRP Construction (Segment A interview). Verbatim. Captures the universal multi-hat reality observed in 27/27 participants, but especially acute for sole operators like Marty where every function is literally one person's job.

## Goals

- Grow profitability, streamline job tracking, attract more clients, and maintain steady work without many administrative tasks.
- Pay his crew and subs accurately and on time without administrative complexity. As he grows, he wants to bring on W-2 employees but dreads the tax and compliance burden that comes with it.
- Understand true labor cost per job for accurate bidding. He currently estimates labor based on gut feel. Knowing the fully burdened cost (wages + employer taxes + workers' comp) per project would make his bids more precise and his margins more predictable.

## Challenges

- Balancing hands-on work with business management, tracking expenses efficiently, handling inconsistent cash flow, and keeping up with administrative tasks without dedicated software.
- The Scale-Up Cliff is directly ahead. Marty wants to hire his first W-2 employee but doesn't know what's required: EIN registration, state tax IDs, SUI, workers' comp, direct deposit setup, W-4 processing, tax deposits. The unknown unknowns are paralyzing.
- 1099 vs. W-2 classification risk. Marty treats everyone as a sub, but some of his long-term helpers may legally qualify as employees. He's unaware of IRS factor tests. Misclassification is an existential financial risk.
- No system for tracking payments to subcontractors. He pays subs from memory or a notebook. At tax time, reconstructing who was paid what for 1099-NEC reporting is a scramble.
- Compliance is invisible until it's a penalty. He doesn't know about quarterly 941 filings, SUTA deposits, or new hire reporting requirements. He'll discover these obligations through a penalty notice, not through proactive awareness.

## Skills

- **Domain expertise**: High -- certified electrician with years of hands-on construction experience. Deep trade knowledge and practical problem-solving ability. Can estimate material costs from experience but lacks formal estimating methodology.
- **Technical proficiency**: Low to Moderate -- uses Excel and Outlook for basic operations. Has looked at software demos (Gusto, QuickBooks Payroll) but was intimidated by setup complexity. Prefers tools that feel like "filling in a form," not navigating a system. Matches the adoption barrier pattern from Findings Theme 10: *"Most of the subcontractors that are single trade, they operate on a very digitally archaic level"* (Chris W./CT West).
- **Financial literacy**: Low -- understands profit and loss at a gut level ("I made money on that job" vs. "I lost money on that one") but does not distinguish between gross wages, fully burdened labor cost, employer tax obligations, or the financial implications of 1099 vs. W-2 classification. Cannot read a P&L statement without his accountant interpreting it.
- **Compliance knowledge**: Very Low -- unaware of IRS factor tests for worker classification, quarterly 941 filing requirements, SUTA deposits, new-hire reporting, or the distinction between state and federal tax obligations. Compliance is entirely delegated to his CPA, who handles it reactively at year-end.

## Habits

- He uses self-made spreadsheets for billing and invoicing but relies on an accountant for taxes and financial fillings.
- Spreadsheets for job tracking and invoices, Quickbooks (via accountant) for taxes, no project management software.
- Pays subcontractors via check or direct bank transfer after each job or at the end of the week. No formal invoice tracking.
- Doesn't distinguish between W-2 and 1099 obligations -- "everyone is a sub" in his mental model.
- His accountant handles 1099-NEC generation at year-end, but Marty often can't provide complete payment records.
- Has looked at Gusto and QuickBooks Payroll websites but was intimidated by the setup process and unsure which features he actually needs.
- Calculates labor cost per job by rough mental math: "I paid the guys about $X for that job" -- no formal tracking of employer-side costs.

## Environment

- **Physical**: Split between field and a home office. Most of the day is spent on job sites -- transporting materials, supervising work, performing hands-on construction. Administrative work happens in the truck (phone calls, texts) or at a desk at home in the evening. There is no dedicated office space. This mirrors the environment described by Shauwn (Absolute Electric): *"Me and my wife, business. I run office accounting"* -- where "office" is wherever Marty happens to be.
- **Technological**: Smartphone (primary device), laptop at home, unreliable internet at job sites. Excel on the laptop, Outlook for email, QuickBooks accessed through his accountant. No project management software, no time tracking tool, no payroll system. The phone is used for photos, texts to subs, and occasional Google searches ("how to put someone on payroll"). Connectivity is intermittent -- cell service at rural or commercial job sites is spotty.
- **Organizational**: No organizational structure. Marty is the entire company. There is no employee handbook, no HR policy, no formal processes. Decisions are instantaneous and undocumented. Subcontractors receive verbal instructions and are paid by check. The "filing system" is a stack of receipts and a notebook. This is the most informal end of the SMB spectrum -- matching Debbie (ATR): *"We've used the same DOS system since 1993"* and Jodi (JRP): *"We do it all internally."*

## Relationships

- **External CPA**: Handles tax preparation at year-end. Marty sends receipts and bank statements; the CPA reconstructs financial records and files returns. The relationship is transactional -- Marty pays for the service but doesn't understand what the CPA does. He trusts the CPA to keep him out of trouble but doesn't know what "trouble" looks like. When asked about taxes: *"They take too much of my money. Other than that..."* (Jodi/JRP -- a direct echo of Marty's attitude).
- **1099 subcontractors (recurring)**: Long-term working relationships with 3-5 subs he trusts and calls repeatedly. These relationships are informal -- no contracts, no formal payment tracking. Some of these subs may legally qualify as W-2 employees under IRS factor tests, but Marty has never considered this distinction. He views them as "my guys," not as a compliance risk.
- **Potential W-2 employee**: A relationship that doesn't exist yet but weighs on Marty's mind. He knows he needs a full-time helper to grow, but the perceived complexity of "real payroll" is a barrier. The imagined relationship with a future employee carries all the anxiety of the unknown.
- **Clients**: Direct, personal relationships. Marty's reputation is built on word-of-mouth. Late payment from clients cascades into his ability to pay subs -- a cash flow chain described by 22/27 interview participants.
- **Spouse**: In the SMB construction pattern, spouses frequently play a bookkeeping or administrative role. *"Submitting the taxes to the government is done by the accountant. Everything else is done by my wife"* (Shauwn/Absolute Electric). Even if Marty doesn't have a spouse doing books, this is the model he'd consider if he scales.

## Motivations

- Marty is driven by independence and craft pride. He started his own business to be his own boss and to work with his hands. Financial and administrative complexity threaten that independence -- they pull him away from the work he values into work he resents.
- He wants steady, profitable work without the overhead of a "real company." His ideal week is 4 days on the job site and a few hours on Friday handling invoices and scheduling next week's work.
- Growth motivation is practical, not aspirational: he wants one or two reliable employees so he can take on larger jobs and stop turning away work. *"Sustaining what we have and making it profitable -- that's my big goal right now"* (Jodi/JRP). He's not building an empire; he's trying to make a living without working 70-hour weeks.
- Being seen as fair and reliable by his crew matters to him. He pays subs the same day or end of the week because that's how he'd want to be treated. Timeliness of payment is a point of personal honor.

## Frustrations

- He doesn't know what he doesn't know about hiring. The Scale-Up Cliff isn't just a compliance challenge -- it's an emotional barrier. Every time he Googles "how to put someone on payroll," the results make him feel like he needs a law degree. The system feels designed to exclude small operators.
- His accountant's $200/month payroll quote feels like a rip-off, but he has no way to evaluate whether it's fair. He can't comparison-shop because he doesn't understand the service he's buying.
- 1099 payment tracking is a mess, and he knows it. At tax time, reconstructing who was paid what from memory and a notebook creates anxiety and arguments with his CPA. *"I just keep pretty close track of everything"* -- but "close" means approximate, not precise (Jodi/JRP).
- Cash flow is unpredictable and stressful. He fronts material and labor costs, then waits 30-60 days for payment. Matching outflows to inflows requires constant mental math that he shouldn't have to do. This is the #1 financial anxiety across all segments (22/27 participants).
- Software demos overwhelm him. He's looked at Gusto and QuickBooks Payroll but couldn't tell which features he actually needs vs. which are upsells. The onboarding experience assumes knowledge he doesn't have. *"I'm very weary of it. Like I've turned down multiple softwares just this year because all they could do is have me watch a demo"* (Larson/SPSD).

## Scenario

Marty wins a 6-week commercial renovation and needs two extra hands. He brings on a helper he's used before (1099 sub, straightforward) and wants to hire a full-time carpenter who'll work across multiple projects. He Googles "how to put someone on payroll" and is overwhelmed by the results -- W-4, I-9, state registration, workers' comp, tax deposits. He calls his accountant, who quotes $200/month to run payroll through ADP. Marty balks at the cost but sees no alternative. He briefly considers just paying the carpenter as a 1099 sub to avoid the hassle -- unaware that this misclassification could trigger IRS penalties exceeding the cost of the entire project. He feels stuck between the work he wants to do and the system he can't navigate.
