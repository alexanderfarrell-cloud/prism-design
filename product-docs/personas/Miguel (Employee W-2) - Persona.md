## About this persona

- Trade: Carpenter / framer with crane operation certification
- Experience: 12 years in residential and commercial construction. No formal education beyond high school and trade apprenticeship.
- Employers: Has worked for 4 different contractors in the last 6 years. Currently employed by a 15-person GC.
- Likes: Steady work, clear expectations, getting paid correctly and on time, and knowing exactly how much he earned before the check hits.
- Dislikes: Confusing pay stubs, calling the office to ask basic questions, surprises in his paycheck, and paperwork that doesn't make sense.

Miguel is an hourly field worker -- a carpenter and framer who also holds a crane operation certification. He works at 2-3 job sites per week, earning different rates depending on the trade type: $30/hour for framing, $45/hour for crane operation. His weekly hours fluctuate between 35 and 55 depending on the project schedule. When he exceeds 40 hours in a week, his overtime rate should reflect a weighted average of the different rates he worked -- but he's never seen a pay stub that actually shows how that number was calculated.

He has changed employers several times, moving between GCs for better pay, steadier work, or a shorter commute. Each move meant filling out paper W-4s and direct deposit forms, waiting for the office to process them, and hoping the first paycheck would be correct. At his current employer (a Liam-type small GC), every question about his pay routes through the office manager, who may or may not have the answer and often has to call the accountant.

Miguel is on the receiving end of every payroll decision made by the PR Admin, the owner, and Sandra the bookkeeper. He doesn't see the systems they use, the calculations they run, or the tax configurations they manage. He sees one thing: his paycheck. And he verifies it every single week -- mentally tallying his hours, rates, and expected gross before the deposit hits. *"Field workers are mathematicians when it comes to their paycheck"* -- this describes Miguel precisely.

## Quote

> "Field workers are mathematicians when it comes to their paycheck, but they barely can get their time cards in."
>
> -- From foundational interview research, cited in the Pass 1 Field Supervisor journey map. Verbatim. Captures the paradox of the construction field worker: meticulous about pay accuracy, indifferent to administrative process. Miguel can calculate his expected gross pay in his head to within $5 -- but has never voluntarily submitted a timesheet early.

## Goals

- Get paid the right amount, every week, with no surprises. He mentally calculates his expected gross pay before payday and checks it against the deposit. If the numbers don't match, trust erodes immediately.
- Understand what was withheld and why. He doesn't object to taxes or deductions -- he objects to not being able to see the math. A single-line pay stub that says "Net Pay: $1,247.00" tells him nothing about whether it's correct.
- Manage his own direct deposit and withholding without calling the office. Changing his bank account or updating his W-4 after a life event shouldn't require a paper form, a phone call, and a 2-week wait.
- Access his W-2 on his own schedule at tax time. He doesn't want to call his employer in February asking where his W-2 is -- he wants to download it from his phone the day it's available.

## Challenges

- **Pay stub opacity**: His current pay stub shows a single "Regular Pay" line and a single "Overtime" line. He worked at two different rates this week -- $30/hr framing and $45/hr crane -- but the stub doesn't break out which hours were paid at which rate. He can't verify the math because the inputs are hidden.
- **WAOT confusion**: When he works multiple rates and exceeds 40 hours, his overtime rate should be a weighted average. He doesn't know the term "WAOT" but he knows his OT rate should be somewhere between $45 and $67.50, not the flat $45 his stub shows. He suspects he's being underpaid on overtime but can't prove it.
- **No self-service access**: To change his direct deposit, he fills out a paper form, gives it to the office manager, and waits. To update his W-4 after his second child was born, same process. To get a copy of a prior pay stub for a loan application, he calls the office and waits for them to email a PDF. Every interaction requires the admin as an intermediary.
- **Multi-state withholding invisibility**: Last month, his crew worked a 3-week project across the state line. He didn't know this triggered withholding for a different state until he saw a new deduction line on his stub -- unlabeled, unexplained. He still doesn't know if his home-state withholding was adjusted or if he's being double-taxed.
- **Onboarding friction at new employers**: When he started at his current GC, he filled out a paper W-4, wrote his bank info on a form, and handed both to the office manager. His first paycheck was a paper check because direct deposit "wasn't set up yet." His second paycheck had the wrong withholding because the W-4 was entered incorrectly. It took three pay periods before everything was right.
- **Seasonal employment and document access**: He's worked for 4 employers in 6 years. When he needs a W-2 from a prior employer for a tax amendment, he has to call their office -- if they're still in business. He has no independent access to his own historical pay documents.

## Skills

- **Trade expertise**: High -- 12 years of carpentry and framing experience, plus crane certification. He's one of the most experienced field workers on any crew he joins. His skills directly determine his earning potential and rate differential.
- **Technical proficiency**: Low -- smartphone is his only device. He can text, use a calculator app, take photos, and browse basic websites. He's not comfortable with apps that require account creation, password management, or multi-step workflows. If it doesn't work in 2 taps, he won't use it. Matches the adoption barrier from Findings Theme 10: *"Most of the subcontractors that are single trade, they operate on a very digitally archaic level"* (Chris W./CT West).
- **Financial literacy**: Low to Moderate -- he understands hours times rate equals gross pay. He knows roughly what federal and state taxes should take out. He cannot explain the difference between FICA and federal income tax, doesn't know what FUTA or SUTA means, and has never heard the term "weighted average overtime." But he can tell you within $5 what his gross should be this week based on the hours he worked.
- **Compliance knowledge**: Very Low -- he doesn't know that working in a different state triggers a withholding change. He doesn't know what a reciprocity agreement is. He filled out his W-4 by copying what he put on the last one. He assumes the employer handles all the tax stuff correctly -- until his paycheck tells him otherwise.

## Habits

- Checks his bank account on Friday morning (or whenever payday is) to confirm the deposit amount. If the number looks wrong, he starts calculating in his head immediately.
- Keeps a mental running tally of his hours and rates throughout the week. By Thursday evening, he has an expected gross number in his head. He doesn't write it down -- it's mental math he's done every week for 12 years.
- When his pay is wrong, he asks his foreman first, then calls the office. If the office can't explain it immediately, his trust drops. Every unresolved pay question compounds -- he remembers every time his check was short.
- Texts his foreman or crew lead about schedule, not the office. The office is a last resort -- he interacts with it only when something is wrong or when paperwork is required.
- Loses paper documents. W-4 forms, direct deposit slips, tax forms -- if it's paper, it gets left in the truck, goes through the wash, or ends up in a pile he never looks at again.
- Files his taxes at a chain tax prep service (H&R Block or equivalent) in February. He brings whatever documents he has and relies on the preparer to sort it out. If his W-2 is late or missing, it delays his refund -- which he depends on.

## Environment

- **Physical**: Job sites -- residential and commercial construction. He spends 8-10 hours a day outdoors or in unfinished buildings. There is no desk, no computer, no printer. His "office" is the cab of his truck, where he eats lunch and checks his phone. He may work at 2-3 different sites in a single week, and job sites change every few weeks as projects complete.
- **Technological**: Android smartphone -- his only computing device. He uses it for texts, calls, photos, a calculator, YouTube (how-to videos), and occasional web browsing. He does not own a laptop or tablet. Cell service at job sites is unreliable -- rural sites may have no signal, commercial sites may have spotty coverage. He has a personal email (Gmail) that he checks sporadically. Any system that requires him to be at a desk, use a desktop browser, or install specialized software has already failed for him.
- **Organizational**: Miguel is at the bottom of the organizational chart for payroll purposes. He has no visibility into the payroll system, no access to his own records, and no channel to manage his own information except through the office. He interacts with the payroll system exclusively through its outputs: his paycheck and his pay stub. Every other interaction is mediated by the admin, the foreman, or paper forms.

## Relationships

- **Foreman / crew lead**: The most important daily relationship. The foreman assigns work, approves time, and is Miguel's first point of contact for any issue -- including pay questions. The foreman can't answer pay questions but often tries, creating confusion. When time entries are wrong, it's usually because the foreman coded them to the wrong job or trade.
- **PR Admin / office manager**: Miguel's only interface to the payroll system. He calls the office when his pay is wrong, when he needs a form, or when he needs a document. The admin is often busy, sometimes unavailable, and may not have the answer without checking with the accountant. Every interaction feels like an imposition -- Miguel doesn't want to bother the office, but he has no alternative.
- **Employer (owner)**: A distant relationship. Miguel may see the owner at the job site occasionally but doesn't interact with them about pay. He evaluates the employer by one metric: does the check come on time and is it correct? If the answer is yes consistently, he stays. If it's not, he starts looking. *"Through this whole process of the next 10 years, we want them to be treated fairly and right and properly"* (Alan/Willow) -- this is the promise Miguel is silently evaluating every payday.
- **Co-workers**: Crew members compare pay informally. "What did you get this week?" conversations happen in the truck. Discrepancies between workers who did the same hours on the same job create immediate suspicion -- even when the difference is explained by different withholding elections.
- **Tax preparer**: An annual relationship. Miguel brings his W-2 (if he has it) to H&R Block in February. The preparer asks questions Miguel can't always answer: "Did you work in another state this year?" "Do you have any 1099 income?" He depends on his employer to have generated correct documents.
- **Prior employers**: Ghost relationships. Miguel has worked for 4 contractors in 6 years. He may need W-2s, pay records, or employment verification from any of them. If the company is still around, he calls the office and hopes someone can find the records. If not, he's out of luck.

## Motivations

- Miguel is motivated by fairness and reliability. He shows up, does skilled work, and expects to be compensated accurately for every hour at the correct rate. Pay accuracy is not a financial abstraction -- it's a measure of whether his employer respects him.
- He values stability but will leave for better pay or steadier work. Loyalty in construction is conditional: it lasts exactly as long as the checks are right and the work is consistent. *"Getting trained and qualified plumbers is always, has been a challenge"* (Chris/Power Plumbing) -- Miguel is the skilled worker every contractor is trying to retain.
- He wants to understand his own pay without needing someone to explain it to him. He doesn't want to learn payroll -- he wants a pay stub that shows the math clearly enough that he can verify it himself in 30 seconds on his phone.
- His tax refund matters. He adjusts his withholding to maximize his refund, which he uses for large expenses (truck repair, tools, family needs). A late or incorrect W-2 directly impacts his annual financial plan.

## Frustrations

- "My pay stub doesn't show me what I actually worked." He worked 32 hours framing and 12 hours on crane this week. His stub says "Regular: 40 hrs, OT: 4 hrs." He can't verify that the framing hours were paid at $30 and the crane hours at $45. The math is hidden behind a summary that assumes one rate.
- His overtime rate looks wrong but he can't prove it. He worked two rates this week and expects OT at roughly $35/hr (the weighted average). His stub shows OT at $30/hr (his lowest rate). He thinks he's being shorted but can't point to the specific line item because the stub doesn't show the WAOT calculation. This matches the Pay Stubs PRD problem statement: *"WAOT overtime is calculated in the background; the stub doesn't show how the overtime rate was derived, creating trust issues."*
- Changing his bank account took 3 weeks. He got a new bank account after a fraud incident. He filled out a paper form, turned it in on Monday. By Friday, his check went to the old account. The next week, same thing -- "it takes a pay cycle to process." He didn't get paid to the correct account for almost a month. He had to borrow money in the interim.
- He can't get his own W-2. His employer from two years ago closed their office. He needs that W-2 for a tax amendment but has no way to access it. He spent 3 hours on the phone with the IRS trying to get a wage transcript. He doesn't understand why he can't just have a copy of his own document.
- Nobody told him he was working in another state. His crew worked across the state line for three weeks. He didn't notice until a new line item appeared on his stub -- "OH State Tax" alongside his usual state. He doesn't know if his home state withholding was reduced, if he's being taxed twice, or if he needs to file in both states at year-end. Nobody explained it. The Employee Management PRD (E2) describes exactly this scenario: *"When a new job site is assigned to an employee in a state not yet configured for that employee"* -- Miguel is the person this happens to.
- Onboarding at a new employer is paper-driven and error-prone. He filled out the same W-4 for the fourth time in six years -- by hand, on a paper form, with no guidance on what "multiple jobs adjustment" means. The first paycheck had the wrong withholding. He knows the drill: it takes 2-3 pay periods before everything is correct at a new job.

## Scenario

Miguel finishes a 48-hour week -- 32 hours framing at $30/hr on a residential project (Job 101), and 16 hours operating a crane at $45/hr on a commercial site (Job 102) across the state line. He expects his gross to be around $1,680 for the 40 regular hours plus roughly $140 for the 8 hours of overtime at a weighted average rate. On Friday morning, he checks his bank account: the deposit is $1,190. He opens the emailed pay stub on his phone. It shows: "Regular Pay: $1,320.00 / Overtime: $180.00 / Net Pay: $1,190.47." He can't tell which hours were paid at which rate. The overtime rate appears to be $22.50/hr -- well below what he expected. He doesn't see any mention of Job 102 or crane operation on the stub.

He calls the office at 7:30 AM. The office manager says she'll look into it and call him back. She doesn't call back until Monday. By then, Miguel has already told three co-workers that "the check was short again." The office manager explains that the overtime was calculated using a different method than Miguel expected, but she can't explain WAOT because she doesn't understand it herself -- she just ran the calculation in QuickBooks. Miguel accepts the explanation but doesn't believe it. He starts browsing job postings on his lunch break.
