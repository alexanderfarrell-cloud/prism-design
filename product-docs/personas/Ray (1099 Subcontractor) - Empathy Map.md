# Empathy Map: Ray (1099 Subcontractor)

## Context

Ray is a 41-year-old independent master plumber who works as a 1099 subcontractor for 3-4 general contractors simultaneously. He is the person on the other side of the W-2/1099 boundary -- the one who receives the payment, the 1099-NEC, and the consequences of every classification decision, data entry error, and year-end scramble that happens inside a GC's payroll system. His engagement with any single GC's system is minimal (onboarding, occasional bank update, 1099-NEC retrieval), but his sensitivity to errors is high. He has no visibility into what any GC's system says about him. He operates on a smartphone, a spiral notebook, and handwritten invoices. Any administrative system he interacts with is competing with "just call the office."

---

## Says

- "I'm a 1099. Don't withhold anything from my check."
- "Every GC has a different way of setting me up. One wants a fax, one wants an email, one asked me to write my bank number on a piece of paper."
- "My check was supposed to be here Friday. It's Monday. Nobody called me to explain why."
- "I don't care what their system says -- I know what I invoiced and I know what I should get paid."
- "Last year, one of my 1099s was three grand off. I had to call them four times to get a corrected one."
- "I don't want to download an app. Just send me the document."
- "I've been doing this for 17 years. Just pay me right and give me my tax form on time."
- "I keep everything in a notebook. If their number doesn't match mine, their number is wrong."
- "The GC that pays on time gets my best availability. The one that's always late gets whoever I have left."
- "My tax guy can't do my return until I have all my 1099s. If one GC is late, everything waits."

## Does

- Records every job, invoice, and payment in a spiral notebook kept in his truck. One notebook per year. This is his primary financial record and his only cross-GC view of his own income.
- Checks his bank account on expected payment days. If payment doesn't arrive within 2 days of expected date, he calls the GC's office directly -- he doesn't send emails, he doesn't submit tickets, he calls.
- Provides the same information (EIN, bank account, business name, address) to every new GC in whatever format they require -- fax, email, paper form, text photo. Doesn't question the process, just complies, because fighting the GC's admin process isn't worth the energy.
- Handwrites invoices from a carbon-copy book. Keeps the yellow copy. For GCs that want electronic invoices, he photographs the handwritten copy and texts or emails it.
- Compares every 1099-NEC against his notebook total when they arrive in January. Catches discrepancies by doing the math himself -- adding up every payment entry for that GC across the year.
- Calls the GC's office to update bank information. Doesn't know if there's another way. Hasn't been offered a self-service option.
- Goes to a local CPA in late February with his notebook, all 1099-NECs, and whatever bank statements he remembers to bring. Waits for the last 1099-NEC to arrive before scheduling the appointment.
- Prioritizes GCs by payment reliability when allocating his time. In weeks where multiple GCs need him, the reliable payers get scheduled first.
- Ignores or abandons any digital process that requires more than 2-3 steps. Account creation, email verification, app downloads, and password setup are barriers that cause him to call the office instead.

## Thinks

- "I know exactly what every GC owes me. The question is whether their system knows it."
- "Every year, at least one 1099 is wrong. It's almost expected at this point."
- "I got set up as a W-2 once and it was a nightmare. I'm always a little nervous when I start with a new GC."
- "I shouldn't have to call someone to change my own bank account. But I don't know another way."
- "If they can't pay me on time, they're either disorganized or they're having cash flow problems. Either way, I need to know."
- "The GCs that treat me well keep me. The ones that don't lose me to someone who pays better or faster."
- "My tax preparer charges me more every year because the paperwork is always a mess. It's not even my mess -- it's their mess."
- "I don't want to learn a new system. I want the system to not need me at all except for the bare minimum."
- "Somewhere in their system there's a record of every payment they've made to me this year. I should be able to see it."
- "I wonder if the other subs who work for the same GC have the same payment problems I do, or if it's just me."

## Feels

- **Frustrated** by the inconsistency across GCs. Every contractor he works for has a different onboarding process, a different payment timeline, a different level of competence with tax documents. He has no control over any of it. He adapts to each GC's process because he has to, not because it works.
- **Anxious** at tax time. January through March is a waiting game: waiting for 1099-NECs, waiting to see if the amounts are correct, waiting to find out if a GC even remembers to send one. Until every document is in and verified against his notebook, he can't file. His refund (or his remaining tax liability) is frozen until the last GC delivers.
- **Powerless** because he has zero visibility into any GC's records about him. He doesn't know his YTD total, his classification, or his bank account on file at any contractor he works for. He discovers problems only through their consequences -- a wrong payment, a missing document, an unexpected withholding. He is a passive recipient of administrative decisions he has no input into.
- **Wary** when starting with a new GC. The misclassification incident left a mark. He watches his first payment carefully: is the full amount there? Was anything withheld? Did the bank deposit match the invoice? If the first payment is right, he relaxes slightly. If anything is off, his guard goes up permanently with that GC.
- **Valued but invisible**. He knows GCs need him -- the labor shortage is real, and licensed plumbers are hard to find. He feels the leverage of being in demand. But inside the GC's administrative system, he's an afterthought -- a record in a list, a 1099-NEC to generate at year-end, a payment to process when someone remembers. The contrast between his market value and his administrative invisibility is something he feels even if he can't articulate it.
- **Resigned** to paper-and-phone processes. He has internalized that "this is how it works" with small GCs. Digital onboarding, self-service portals, and automated 1099 delivery are things that exist in theory but not in his actual experience. He doesn't expect better -- which means the bar for delighting him is surprisingly low.
- **Protective** of his independence. He chose to be a 1099 contractor. He manages his own schedule, his own clients, his own taxes. Any system that feels like it's treating him as an employee -- withholding taxes, requiring regular logins, sending notifications he didn't ask for -- triggers resistance. He wants to be a guest in the system, not a resident.

---

## Goals and Needs

**At the center of everything Ray wants is this: get paid correctly, get his tax documents on time, and be left alone in between.**

- A fast, standardized onboarding process that captures his information once, digitally, with validation -- so his EIN, bank account, and classification are right from the first payment. No faxes, no sticky notes, no retyping.
- Correct classification as a 1099 contractor, enforced by the system. He should never be accidentally enrolled in withholding. The classification guardrail should protect him as much as it protects the GC.
- Payment confirmation when a payment is sent -- a simple notification (text or email) that says "Payment of $X was sent on [date] and should arrive by [date]." He doesn't need a dashboard. He needs to stop wondering.
- Self-service access to his 1099-NEC via the simplest possible path: a secure link in a text or email, one tap, PDF download. No account creation, no app, no password. The document should be available by January 15 -- well before the January 31 deadline.
- Visibility into his YTD payment total at each GC, accessible from his phone, so he can verify the 1099-NEC amount before it's even generated. If the system total doesn't match his notebook, he wants to flag it before year-end -- not after.
- A persistent record of his 1099-NECs across employers and years, accessible even if a GC closes or he stops working for them. His tax documents should belong to him, not to the GC's system.

## Analysis

### Says vs. Thinks Gap

Ray says "just pay me right" but thinks "I suspect I'm being tracked incorrectly in their system and I have no way to verify it." The simplicity of what he says masks a deeper concern: he has no audit trail into the systems that determine his income records. He keeps his own books in a notebook because he doesn't trust that anyone else's records are accurate -- and his experience has validated this distrust (the $3,000 1099-NEC discrepancy, the misclassification incident).

He says "I don't want to use a portal" but thinks "I should be able to see what they have on me." The resistance isn't to self-service -- it's to the friction of self-service as he's experienced it (account creation, app downloads, multi-step verification). If access were frictionless (a link, a tap, a document), he'd use it. His rejection of "the portal" is a rejection of the implementation, not the concept.

He tells GCs "my payment was late" but doesn't tell them "I'm deprioritizing you next time I have a scheduling conflict." Like Miguel, Ray's attrition signal is invisible to the GC. The GC doesn't know that late payments translate directly into reduced availability from their best subs -- until the sub stops returning calls.

### Key Tensions

1. **Minimal engagement vs. high-consequence interactions**: Ray touches the GC's system maybe 5 times a year. But each interaction carries disproportionate weight: a wrong classification triggers IRS exposure, a wrong bank account means a bounced payment, a wrong 1099-NEC means an incorrect tax return. The system must get these few interactions right every time -- there's no volume of positive interactions to offset a single failure.

2. **Independence vs. dependency**: Ray chose independence -- his own LLC, his own schedule, his own clients. But he's completely dependent on GCs' administrative systems for payment processing, tax document generation, and record accuracy. He controls the work; he controls nothing about the payment and documentation infrastructure. This is the fundamental tension of the 1099 relationship.

3. **Analog user vs. digital system**: Ray operates on paper and phone calls. The system that manages his payments and tax documents is digital. The gap between his tools and the GC's tools creates friction at every interface point: onboarding (paper W-9 into digital system), payment confirmation (no digital notification), 1099-NEC delivery (mailed paper vs. portal). Closing this gap requires meeting Ray where he is (text/SMS, simple links, PDF downloads), not where the system wants him to be (accounts, apps, dashboards).

4. **GC's "low reporting responsibility" vs. Ray's high documentary need**: Jeff from the research says *"you don't have much of reporting responsibility"* for 1099 subs. But Ray needs every payment tracked accurately for his own taxes. The GC's perception of low responsibility and Ray's need for high accuracy are in direct conflict -- and Ray pays the price when the GC's records are wrong.

5. **Multiple GC clients, zero interoperability**: Ray works for 3-4 GCs, each with separate administrative systems. There is no interoperability between them. He can't see a unified view of his income across GCs. He can't update his bank account once and have it propagate. Each GC is a silo, and Ray manually bridges them with his notebook. A platform that multiple GCs use could, in principle, give Ray a single identity across employers -- but only if the product is designed with the sub's cross-GC experience in mind, not just the GC's single-company view.

### Design Implications

1. **1099 onboarding must be standardized, digital, and classification-safe**: The onboarding wizard's 1099 path (E2) should be the first moment of trust between Ray and the system. Digital W-9 capture, EIN validation, bank account verification (routing number check), and the IRS factor classification guardrail must all happen before the first payment. If Ray's first experience with the system is "they got everything right on the first try," his perception of the GC improves -- and by extension, of the platform. This is a competitive differentiator: Gusto's "Contractor Only" tier at $35/mo + $6/contractor shows the market recognizes 1099 sub management as a standalone product need.

2. **Payment confirmation via SMS or email -- not a portal login**: When a payment is disbursed to Ray, the system should send a text or email: "Payment of $4,800 was sent on March 14. Expected arrival: March 16." This single notification eliminates Ray's primary weekly frustration (checking his bank account and wondering). It requires no login, no app, no account. It meets him on the device he already uses (phone) through the channel he already checks (text). The ESS Portal PRD (E7b) identifies 1099 subs as tertiary users -- the system should serve them through notifications, not through portal engagement.

3. **1099-NEC delivery via secure link, zero friction**: The 1099-NEC should arrive as a text or email with a secure, time-limited link that opens directly to a PDF download. No account creation. No app. No password. One tap, one document. The Tax Filing features (E8-4) describe digital delivery and the user story is explicit: *"receive my 1099-NEC digitally by January 31 via a secure email link so that I can file my taxes on time without chasing the GC for paperwork."* For Ray, the bar is even lower: if it takes more than 2 taps, he'll call the office.

4. **YTD payment visibility for subs -- pre-1099 verification**: The ESS Portal should give Ray read access to his YTD payment total at each GC -- a single number he can check against his notebook at any time, but especially in Q4 before the 1099-NEC is generated. If the system total doesn't match his records, he can flag the discrepancy in November instead of discovering it in February after the document is filed. This shifts 1099-NEC accuracy from a year-end scramble to a continuous, low-effort verification process. Access should be via the same secure-link-no-account pattern as the 1099-NEC itself.

5. **Classification guardrails protect the sub, not just the GC**: The IRS factor classification wizard in E2 is framed in the PRDs as a GC compliance tool. But from Ray's perspective, it's a protection mechanism. The system should visually confirm to the sub during onboarding: "You have been classified as a 1099 independent contractor. No taxes will be withheld from your payments." This simple confirmation addresses Ray's deepest onboarding anxiety -- the fear that he'll be set up as a W-2 again. The AI classification confidence scoring (E11-4) adds a layer of ongoing protection: if a GC's relationship with a sub starts looking more like employment, the system flags it before an audit does.

6. **Persistent sub identity across GCs**: If multiple GCs use the platform, Ray should have a single identity that follows him. His EIN, bank account, and contact information should be entered once and reusable when a new GC adds him. His 1099-NECs from all GCs should be accessible in one place. His payment history should span employers. This transforms the product from a GC-centric tool into a sub-centric record system -- and it's the feature that would most fundamentally change Ray's experience. Today, each GC is a silo; his notebook is the only unified view. A persistent sub identity eliminates the notebook as a necessary workaround.

7. **Design for the lowest common denominator of digital fluency**: Every sub-facing feature must be tested against Ray's reality: smartphone only, sporadic email checking, no app willingness, unreliable connectivity at job sites. SMS is the primary reliable channel. PDFs are the universally understood document format. Links should load fast on mobile data, not require Wi-Fi. The research is explicit: *"Most of the subcontractors that are single trade, they operate on a very digitally archaic level."* The system succeeds with Ray when it's invisible -- when it delivers the right information through the simplest possible channel at the right time, without asking anything of him in return.
