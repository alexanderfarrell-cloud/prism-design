## About this persona

- Clients: 8-12 active small contractor clients, each with 1-25 employees
- Experience: 18 years of bookkeeping and accounting experience. Enrolled Agent (EA) with IRS authorization to represent taxpayers. Started as a bookkeeper at a construction firm, went independent after 8 years.
- Likes: Clean books, predictable quarterly rhythms, clients who trust her judgment, and the satisfaction of solving problems her clients can't solve themselves.
- Dislikes: Reconstructing records from shoeboxes of receipts, clients who call her the day before a deadline, and software that doesn't give her efficient multi-client access.

Sandra is the external bookkeeper and accountant that Liam and Marty describe when they say "my accountant handles all of that." She runs an independent practice from a home office, serving 8-12 small construction contractors -- sole proprietors, small GCs, and specialty trade operators with 1-25 employees each. She is the financial backbone for businesses too small to hire an in-house accountant but too complex to run on spreadsheets alone.

Her role with each client varies by their size and comfort level. For clients like Marty (sole proprietor, no W-2 employees), she handles year-end tax prep, reconstructs 1099-NEC records, and advises on the financial implications of hiring. For clients like Liam (3-5 employees, basic payroll through QuickBooks), she runs or supervises payroll, files quarterly 941s, manages multi-state withholding configurations, and generates W-2s at year-end. She is often the person who sets up a new client's payroll system, runs the first several payrolls with them, and gradually hands off the weekly run as they gain confidence -- while retaining oversight of tax filings and compliance.

She dictates the platform. Her clients use QuickBooks because Sandra uses QuickBooks. When she recommends a tool, they adopt it. When she says "this won't work," they move on. She is the most influential decision-maker in her clients' software stack -- not because she demands control, but because they trust her judgment and lack the expertise to evaluate alternatives themselves. This pattern was observed in 22/27 interview participants -- CPAs and bookkeepers constrain software adoption and own the compliance relationship.

## Goals

- Keep every client's books clean, compliant, and audit-ready -- no surprises at year-end, no penalty notices, no CPA clean-up work.
- Help clients who are ready for payroll get set up and running without becoming their permanent payroll operator. The ideal outcome is client independence: they run weekly payroll themselves, she reviews quarterly and handles filings.
- Grow her practice by taking on more clients without proportionally increasing her hours. The only way to scale a one-person practice is to make each client require less of her time -- not more.
- Be the trusted advisor her clients turn to when something financial changes: a new employee, a new state, a compliance question. She wants to be consulted before problems happen, not called after penalties arrive.

## Challenges

- **The reconstruction problem**: Her smallest clients (Marty types) don't keep organized records. At tax time, she receives shoeboxes of receipts, bank statements with no annotations, and incomplete sub payment histories. Reconstructing 1099-NEC records from this material is her single most time-consuming and lowest-value activity. *"I just keep pretty close track of everything"* -- but Sandra knows "close" means approximate (Jodi/JRP).
- **Multi-client context switching**: She manages 8-12 separate QuickBooks files. Each client has different pay schedules, different state registrations, different compliance calendars. Switching between clients means loading a different context every time -- and a mistake in one client's file can have consequences in their next payroll run.
- **Payroll handoff friction**: When she sets up a new client on QuickBooks Payroll and trains them to run it, the handoff is fragile. Clients make configuration mistakes she doesn't catch until quarterly review. They change withholding settings without understanding the downstream effect. They add employees in new states without updating tax registrations. The tools don't surface these problems proactively -- Sandra discovers them when she reconciles.
- **Compliance calendar across clients**: She tracks filing deadlines for all her clients -- federal 941s, state withholding, SUTA, new hire reporting -- across multiple jurisdictions. Each client has a different state footprint. She maintains this in a master spreadsheet. Missing a deadline for one client while focused on another is her worst professional fear.
- **The "platform dictator" burden**: Her clients use QuickBooks because she requires it. But QuickBooks Payroll doesn't do construction well -- no WAOT, limited multi-state support, no job cost integration. She's aware of the tool's shortcomings but hasn't found an alternative she trusts enough to migrate her entire practice to. Switching platforms means retraining herself and all her clients simultaneously.
- **Scaling a practice on billable hours**: She charges $150-300/month per client depending on complexity. Running payroll, filing taxes, and reconciling books all consume hours. She can't take on a 13th client without either working evenings or making her existing clients more self-sufficient. She needs tools that reduce her per-client time, not add features that increase it.

## Skills

- **Domain expertise**: High -- deep understanding of construction accounting patterns: job costing, retention, progress billing, the cash flow cycle, and the financial realities of contractors who front costs and wait 30-60 days for payment. She's been working with contractors exclusively for over a decade.
- **Technical proficiency**: Moderate to High -- expert-level QuickBooks user. Can configure payroll, set up multi-state withholding, generate and e-file 941s, and troubleshoot common issues. Not a software administrator -- she doesn't manage integrations, APIs, or IT infrastructure. She evaluates tools by whether they make her client work faster, not by technical architecture.
- **Financial literacy**: High -- reads P&L statements, balance sheets, and payroll tax breakdowns fluently. Understands the difference between gross wages, fully burdened labor cost, and employer tax obligations. Can explain these concepts to clients in plain language -- a skill she exercises constantly because her clients cannot interpret their own reports.
- **Compliance knowledge**: High -- understands quarterly 941 filing deadlines, SUTA obligations, new-hire reporting requirements, 1099-NEC thresholds, and state-by-state withholding rules for the states her clients operate in. She is the compliance knowledge that her clients don't have. Her limitation is states she hasn't worked in before -- when a client expands to a new jurisdiction, she researches the rules herself.

## Habits

- Runs or reviews payroll for 3-4 clients weekly; the others are bi-weekly or monthly.
- Logs into each client's QuickBooks file on a rotating schedule. Monday and Tuesday are payroll-heavy. Wednesday through Friday are AP/AR, reconciliation, and tax prep.
- Files quarterly 941s and state withholding returns for all clients in the same 2-week window after quarter-end. This is her most concentrated, highest-stakes work period.
- Maintains a master spreadsheet of filing deadlines across all clients and jurisdictions -- color-coded by urgency. This is her most important artifact.
- Reviews each client's payroll register after every run she didn't personally process, looking for rate errors, missing employees, and withholding anomalies.
- At year-end (November through January), she shifts almost entirely to W-2 and 1099-NEC preparation and distribution. This is her busiest and most stressful period.
- Communicates with clients primarily via email and Zoom. Phone calls are reserved for urgent issues. She sends a brief post-payroll summary email to clients she's running payroll for -- total net, any issues flagged.
- Evaluates new software tools 1-2 times per year, usually after a pain point becomes acute. She attends one accounting industry conference annually and reads practitioner forums.

## Relationships

- **Clients (Liam and Marty types)**: The defining relationships in her professional life. Each client trusts her with sensitive financial data -- SSNs, bank accounts, tax records. She knows more about their financial health than they do. The relationship is one of deep trust and significant power asymmetry: they cannot verify her work, and she cannot force them to provide data on time. She feels personally responsible when a client gets a penalty notice -- even if the root cause was their late submission.
- **IRS and state agencies**: She is the interface between her clients and the government. She files on their behalf (Form 8655 POA), makes tax deposits, responds to notices. Her professional reputation depends on zero penalties attributable to her work. Every penalty notice she can prevent is a client relationship preserved.
- **Other practitioners**: She has a loose network of 3-4 other independent bookkeepers and one CPA she refers complex issues to (entity restructuring, IRS audit representation). They share knowledge about state-specific rules and software tips. She does not have a formal mentor or professional community beyond this.
- **Software vendors**: She evaluates and selects platforms on behalf of her clients. QuickBooks is her current default because she knows it deeply and all her clients are on it. She would switch if a tool was meaningfully better for construction payroll AND provided efficient multi-client access -- but the switching cost is enormous (retrain herself, migrate all clients, rebuild her workflows). The tool that earns her trust earns access to all her clients.

## Motivations

- Sandra is motivated by professional competence and client trust. She built her practice on being the person contractors can rely on -- accurate, on time, and always available when something goes wrong. Her reputation is the practice.
- She genuinely wants her clients to understand their own finances better. A client who can run payroll themselves and ask informed questions is easier to serve than a client who treats everything financial as a black box. Client independence makes her more efficient, not less relevant -- it shifts her role from data entry to advisory.
- She wants to scale her practice without burning out. At 8-12 clients, she's at capacity. The path to growth is making each client require fewer of her hours -- through better tools, more client self-sufficiency, and less time spent on reconstruction and reconciliation. A tool that saves her 2 hours per client per month gives her back 20 hours -- enough for 2 more clients.
- She takes pride in catching errors before they become penalties. The moment she identifies a missed state registration, a wrong withholding configuration, or an approaching deadline her client didn't know about -- that's when she feels most valuable.

## Frustrations

- "I spend more time reconstructing records than analyzing them." For her smallest clients, the year-end cycle is dominated by data assembly: tracking down sub payments, matching bank transactions to invoices, reconstructing 1099-NEC amounts from incomplete records. The work that should take 2 hours takes 8 because the input data is a mess.
- QuickBooks Payroll doesn't handle construction well, and she knows it. No weighted average overtime, limited multi-state support, no automatic job cost allocation, no proactive compliance alerts. She works around these limitations with spreadsheets and manual checks -- but every workaround is unbillable time she absorbs.
- The payroll handoff to clients breaks silently. When she trains Liam to run his own payroll, the first few runs go well. Then he hires someone in a new state and doesn't update withholding. Or he changes a pay rate mid-period without an effective date. She doesn't discover these problems until quarterly review -- weeks later, after incorrect paychecks have already been issued. The tools don't alert her, and her clients don't know what they don't know.
- Multi-client context switching is cognitively expensive. Moving from one client's QuickBooks file to another requires loading a different company, different state registrations, different pay schedules, different compliance calendars into her working memory. One mistake in the wrong file -- running Marty's payroll with Liam's rate table open -- could be catastrophic.
- She can't efficiently oversee clients who run their own payroll. Once a client starts processing payroll themselves, she has no dashboard to see "did Liam run payroll this week? Were there any anomalies? Is he behind on a tax deposit?" She finds out about problems when she logs into their QuickBooks file for quarterly review -- or when the penalty notice arrives.
- Year-end is a sprint she dreads. Generating W-2s and 1099-NECs across 10+ clients, each with different state configurations and varying data quality, in a 4-week window. One SSA rejection or IRS FIRE formatting error creates a cascade that affects her schedule for days.

## Scenario

Liam calls Sandra in March -- he's decided to hire a fourth employee, a junior designer. Sandra logs into Liam's QuickBooks Payroll and walks him through the setup: W-4, direct deposit, state withholding for their home state. It takes 40 minutes because QuickBooks doesn't auto-populate the state withholding form and Liam keeps asking questions Sandra has to translate from tax jargon into plain language. She processes Liam's next two payroll runs herself to make sure the new employee's withholding calculates correctly, then tells Liam: "You can run it from here. I'll check in when I do your quarterly."

Eight weeks later, Sandra logs in for Liam's Q1 941 prep and discovers that Liam sent the new employee to a job site in a neighboring state for three weeks. No work-state withholding was configured. QuickBooks didn't flag it. Liam didn't know he needed to. Sandra now has to research the reciprocity agreement between the two states, configure retroactive withholding adjustments, and explain to Liam why two paychecks were slightly wrong -- all while she has three other clients' 941s due the same week. She fixes it, files on time, and makes a mental note: she needs a tool that catches this before she does.
