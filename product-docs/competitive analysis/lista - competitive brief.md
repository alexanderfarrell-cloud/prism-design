# Competitive Brief: Construction Payroll for SMB Contractors

## The Competitive Set

Your competitors fall into four tiers, each representing a different level of threat and a different lesson for your product:

### Direct Competitors (same problem, same users)

These are purpose-built construction payroll platforms targeting SMB contractors. They'll show up in every deal evaluation.

**Lumber** — The AI-forward challenger. Positions as the modern, automated alternative for construction payroll. Handles union fringes, prevailing wage, certified payroll, and multi-state calculations. Claims 30-minute weekly payroll processing. Strongest in union compliance automation. Pricing is per-employee/month, custom quoted.

**Miter** — The "modern HCM" for construction. Covers payroll, HR, time tracking, expenses, and field ops in one platform. Trusted by 1,000+ construction companies. Pricing ranges $15–$50/user/month. Known weakness: limited support for the most complex multi-state, multi-locality, and multi-trade scenarios — exactly the domain you're targeting.

**Payroll4Construction** — The specialist incumbent. Has been serving this niche for 15+ years with deep loyalty among prevailing-wage contractors. Handles union fringes, multi-state, and certified payroll. Pricing is custom-quoted. Strong reputation but dated UX (desktop-optimized, not mobile-first).

### Indirect Competitors (same problem, different approach)

**Foundation Software** — A full construction accounting suite with payroll as a module. Targets contractors with $1M–$250M revenue and 10–1,500 employees. Pricing starts at ~$500/month. Very capable but heavyweight — requires accounting knowledge and significant setup. This is the "ERP approach" to the problem.

**Sage Construction (100 Contractor / Intacct)** — The legacy enterprise player. Deep functionality but priced for mid-to-large firms ($25K–$100K range for full implementations). Overkill for your target SMB audience but sets feature expectations for contractors who've used it at larger firms.

### Adjacent Competitors (could expand into your space)

**Gusto** — Easy-to-use SMB payroll loved for simplicity, but lacks construction-specific features (no job costing, no certified payroll, limited union support). If they added a "construction mode," they'd be a serious threat given their UX advantage.

**Rippling** — Powerful all-in-one HR/payroll platform with growing industry verticalization. Multi-state compliance is strong. No deep construction specialization today, but they have the engineering muscle to build it.

**QuickBooks Payroll** — Already lives on many contractors' desktops via QuickBooks accounting. Simple payroll for simple needs. The "good enough" option that stops SMBs from seeking specialized tools.

### Substitute Solutions (doing nothing / manual)

Many SMB contractors still outsource payroll to local bookkeepers or use spreadsheets + a generic payroll service. This is arguably your biggest "competitor" — the inertia of manual processes plus a trusted accountant.

## Positioning Analysis

Each competitor occupies a distinct message territory:

| Competitor | Category Claim | Differentiator | Promise | Who It's Really For |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lumber** | "AI-powered construction payroll" | AI anomaly detection, automation | "Process payroll in 30 minutes" | Tech-forward contractors, 20–200 employees |
| **Miter** | "Modern construction HCM" | Unified platform (payroll + HR + field ops) | "Reduce payroll time by 85%" | Growing contractors wanting one system |
| **Payroll4Construction** | "Construction payroll simplified" | 15+ years specialization, deep knowledge | "Experts who understand your business" | Prevailing wage / union shops |
| **Foundation** | "Construction accounting suite" | Full ERP with payroll module | "Manage profitable jobs" | Established contractors with admin teams |
| **Gusto** | "Easy payroll for small business" | Consumer-grade UX | "Payroll in minutes, not hours" | General SMBs, simple construction use cases |

### Positioning Gaps — Your Opportunity

Three unclaimed positions emerge:

1. **"Zero-learning-curve construction payroll"** — Nobody owns the explicit message of "you don't need to understand payroll rules." Lumber hints at automation, but still surfaces complexity in their UI. You can own the position of the tool that *truly* abstracts away the complexity.

2. **"Embedded payroll for your existing workflow"** — Every competitor is a standalone system requiring another login, another integration, another data sync. Being built into the finance platform your users already trust is a structural moat.

3. **"Construction payroll for the non-accountant"** — The persona most competitors design for is the construction office administrator who already understands payroll concepts. Your persona — the busy contractor who does their own books — is underserved.

### Crowded Positions to Avoid

Don't compete on "comprehensive" or "all-in-one." That territory is claimed by Miter, Foundation, and Sage. Don't lead with "AI-powered" — Lumber owns that narrative and it's becoming table-stakes language.

## Strategic Implications for Your Product

### Where you win

Your "Complexity Abstraction" philosophy targets the single biggest pain point across this entire market. Research consistently shows that construction software suffers from UX designed for desktop power users, not field workers. Every competitor review surfaces the same theme: *powerful but hard to learn, hard to use, requires expertise.*

Your structural advantages:

- **Embedded in the platform** — No new system to adopt. The payroll feature lives where contractors already manage finances. This dramatically reduces the switching cost that keeps SMBs stuck on manual processes or generic tools.
- **SMB-native simplicity** — Foundation and Sage are too heavy. Lumber and Miter still assume some payroll literacy. You can design for the contractor who thinks in "how much do I owe my crew" not "what are the fringe allocation offsets."
- **Complexity handled invisibly** — Multi-state reciprocity and weighted average overtime are hard problems that competitors expose to the user through configuration screens. If you can compute these correctly without requiring the user to understand them, that's a genuine moat.

### Where you're vulnerable

- **Feature depth perception** — Buyers who come from Foundation or Sage may perceive simplicity as "not enough." You'll need to demonstrate that the calculations happening behind the scenes are as rigorous as what they'd configure manually elsewhere.
- **Trust gap on compliance** — Payroll errors carry real penalties. Contractors will need strong reassurance that the "magic" behind the simple interface is audit-grade. Consider visible compliance indicators ("✓ Davis-Bacon compliant") that build confidence without requiring understanding.
- **Job costing and time tracking** — These are table-stakes features in this market. If your platform doesn't cover them natively, you'll need tight integrations or partners.

### Recommended Strategic Moves

1. **Lead with the persona, not the feature list.** Your messaging should start with "built for contractors who don't have a payroll department" — not with a checklist of capabilities. Let competitors fight over feature matrices; you own the simplicity narrative.

2. **Make compliance visible but not configurable.** Show users that their payroll is compliant (green checkmarks, audit reports, plain-language summaries) without requiring them to configure compliance rules. This is the UX translation of "Complexity Abstraction."

3. **Invest in guided onboarding.** The biggest competitor to any new payroll system is inertia. A setup flow that asks "what states do you work in?" and "are any of your workers union?" then auto-configures everything is worth more than any single feature.

4. **Build switching narratives for each competitor.** Different competitors require different "why switch" stories: from Gusto = "you've outgrown generic payroll"; from Foundation/Sage = "you shouldn't need a degree to run payroll"; from manual/bookkeeper = "stop paying someone to do what software should handle."
